The instruction at the end of Liber Primus's first koan can be compared with the White Queen's impossible-things speech in Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass. The comparison concerns a numbered practice that challenges ordinary expectations. It is a possible literary association, not an established quotation or source attribution.

Compare the actual words

SourceWording
LP1-09, contextual EnglishDO FOUR UNREASONABLE THINGS EACH DAY
Carroll, chapter V, “Wool and Water”Why, sometimes I've believed as many as six impossible things before breakfast.

Carroll's sentence is spoken by the White Queen in Through the Looking-Glass. The primary text at Project Gutenberg places it in a conversation about believing something Alice considers impossible. The Queen treats belief as something improved by practice.

The manuscript's literal sequence, after joining its line wrap, is DO-FOUR-UNREASONABLE-THNGS-EACH-DAY. THINGS is contextual English for THNGS under the rune-label convention. Do not silently replace the literal sequence when investigating its numbers. See transliteration and the complete first koan.

The differences matter

  • Action: the manuscript says to do things; the Queen describes believing things.
  • Number: the manuscript gives four; Carroll gives as many as six.
  • Qualifier: unreasonable is not identical to impossible. An action can be considered unreasonable without being logically or physically impossible.
  • Time: the manuscript says each day; Carroll's example is before breakfast.
  • Voice: the manuscript presents a direct instruction, while Carroll gives a character's recollection within a comic dialogue.

Those differences prevent calling the manuscript line a verbatim Carroll quotation. The structural resemblance is worth recording, but it is less specific than the holy-life verbal parallel with Blake. This article does not establish that Carroll was the immediate source of the instruction.

Read the instruction after the story

In LP1-06–09, the master rejects the student's name, occupation, species and description of consciousness as answers to who the student is. Admission to study follows the student's inability to complete another answer. The four-things instruction comes after this exchange, separated as an instruction. A possible reading is that it extends the story's challenge to familiar explanations into a daily exercise. That is editorial interpretation, not an additional decoded message.

Carroll's scene instead concerns Alice's refusal to believe an apparently impossible age and the Queen's response. The two contexts both challenge ordinary judgments, but their immediate questions differ: identity in the koan, belief in the Carroll exchange. A similarity in tone does not demonstrate identical philosophical commitments.
