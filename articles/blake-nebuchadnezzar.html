The narrow strips beside the central compasses panels are identified as fragments of William Blake’s Nebuchadnezzar in Frank G. Bosman’s study of the cover. They are easy to overlook because the collage splits the composition into its margins.

Who is Nebuchadnezzar?

In Tate’s account of Blake’s work, the subject is the biblical king reduced to an animal-like existence as punishment for pride. Tate dates its work to 1795–about 1805 and identifies the medium as colour print with ink and watercolour on paper. The title names the figure in the artwork; it is not a word transcribed from the cover.

The episode in Daniel 4

The narrative behind the subject is more specific than a general image of a fallen king. Read Daniel 4:28–37 in the King James Version; the chapter moves through pride, loss, and restoration:

Verses 28–30: the boast
Nebuchadnezzar attributes Babylon’s greatness to his own power and majesty.
Verses 31–33: the humiliation
A heavenly pronouncement removes his kingdom. He is driven away from people, eats grass, and is exposed to dew. The description compares his hair to eagle feathers and his nails to bird claws.
Verses 34–36: the return
He looks toward heaven, regains understanding, and is restored to his kingdom.
Verse 37: the stated lesson
His closing praise identifies pride as something divine power can bring low.

The hair and nail comparisons provide concrete textual details to inspect against the full artwork. They are similes in this translation, not a statement that the king becomes a bird. The passage also continues beyond the animal-like condition to restoration; the isolated cover fragments do not show that whole sequence.

For the puzzle, keep three steps distinct: the text explains the artwork’s subject; visual correspondence supports identifying the borrowed composition; a further argument would be needed to make Daniel a cipher source. Selecting this translation for a readable reference does not establish that the puzzle authors used it. A book-cipher test would need an edition and counting rule as well as a passage.

Identify the museum object

Tate’s published metadata identifies its Nebuchadnezzar as N05059. An accession number identifies an object in a collection; it is not a date or a manuscript page number. The record gives the following details:

Date
1795–c. 1805; the “c.” means approximately.
Medium
Colour print, ink and watercolour on paper.
Support dimensions
543 × 725 mm, as recorded. These are physical dimensions, not pixel dimensions of an online image.
Acquisition credit
Presented by W. Graham Robertson, 1939.

The accession identifies Tate’s object. It does not establish that this particular impression, or Tate’s digital photograph of it, supplied the puzzle fragment. Keep that question separate from recognizing the composition.

How to inspect the side panels

Begin with the unchanged cover image and the full composition shown by Tate. Follow the outlines of the limbs and hair through each narrow strip. Bosman describes rotated fragments; compare orientation as well as shape. A fragment can become difficult to recognize when a familiar horizontal body is turned into a vertical border.

Record which source reproduction you compare. Different photographs and impressions can differ in colour, contrast and framing. Identifying the composition is a different task from identifying the exact digital image used to assemble the collage.
