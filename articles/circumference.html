Circumference is one of the manuscript’s recurring terms for thinking about identity. In the contextual known readings, it appears in The Loss of Divinity, the second koan, and the final parable. The passages connect behavior, the inner voice, and the shedding of a surrounding identity, but they do not give a formal definition that settles every interpretation.

A pattern of dependence

The Loss of Divinity describes consumption and preservation through fears about having too little or being unable to recover what has been lost. Its criticism of adherence is more specific than a rejection of belief: both dogma and reason can be followed for the sake of belonging and being right. The passage therefore directs attention to the motive for following an idea as well as the idea itself.

The later advice about wealth complicates a simple anti-materialist reading. The text permits accumulation while rejecting attachment to possessions. Read together, these statements support an interpretation in which dependence on possessions and group approval constrains the self. That is an interpretation of the sequence, not a dictionary definition of “circumference.”

Why the second koan matters

The second koan moves from outward behavior to the voice identified as the I. The student thinks that no such voice exists; the master points to that thought as the example. The structure is self-referential: an attempted denial supplies the instance being denied.

This makes the circumference more than a literal physical perimeter in that dialogue. It is associated with a narrated or described identity. Yet the story does not establish that every person experiences thought in the same way, or that a particular psychological theory is its source. The koan guide follows both dialogues and distinguishes their literary structure from a historical attribution.

What changes in the parable

The parable uses the plural, circumferences, beside an image of emergence. Its instar comparison gives shedding a biological counterpart: moving beyond a limiting outer form. The repeated vocabulary makes it reasonable to read this alongside the earlier behavioral and identity passages.

The plural does not itself tell us how many layers there are, what order to remove them in, or which cipher transformation to apply. Those would be additional hypotheses. The text provides a recurring metaphor; a computational proposal still needs an explicit operation.

The geometric word and a numerical hypothesis

In geometry, circumference concerns a circle’s boundary or its length. For a circle of radius r, the length is 2πr. The manuscript’s use of the word does not supply a radius, a unit, or an instruction to calculate π. A solver proposing such a connection must identify which page data represent those quantities.

The same caution applies to “primality” in The Loss of Divinity. Its proximity to the manuscript’s prime-valued alphabet makes a numerical comparison worth stating carefully, but it does not specify whether to use rune indices, prime values, word totals, or something else. The prime-sum guide explains how to make a proposed calculation reproducible.

For an external comparison, see Blake’s outward circumference of energy. The shared term deserves attention, but the two works assign it different roles.
