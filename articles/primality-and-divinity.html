Primality and divinity meet in a specific claim in The Loss of Divinity: consumption, preservation, and adherence cause the loss of primality, and consequently divinity. The later parable describes divinity as something to find within. Reading the passages together gives the words a context; it does not turn them into a complete philosophical system or a cipher instruction.

What the passage actually argues

The sequence matters. Consumption is linked to perceived scarcity and fear of future inability to obtain what is needed. Preservation is linked to fear of being unable to regain what is lost. Adherence is linked to wanting to belong and be right. The text treats both dogma and reason as things that can be followed for that last motive. Its argument concerns dependence on possession and approval, rather than simply listing disfavored objects or beliefs.

This table follows the passage’s argument, not an experimentally established causal chain. The text does not define a measurable quantity called divinity. Nor does its advice reduce to giving up possessions: the same section advises accumulating wealth while avoiding attachment to it. That distinction makes autonomy or freedom from dependence a plausible reading of what is being lost.

What primality means mathematically

An integer is prime when it is greater than one and has exactly two positive divisors: one and itself. For example, 13 has only 1 and 13; 15 also has 3 and 5, so 15 is composite. One is neither prime nor composite. “Indivisible” is a useful shorthand only if it means that there is no factorization into two integers both greater than one.

Gematria Primus assigns the first 29 primes to its 29 runes. Every individual rune value is therefore prime by construction. That does not make a word total prime: the F and U values, 2 and 3, sum to 5, while U and TH, 3 and 5, sum to 8. A property of each term need not survive addition.

There is thus a real mathematical context for the manuscript’s choice of vocabulary. But the statement about losing primality does not identify a number, a sum, or an operation. Moving from its wording to a calculation requires a further proposal. The prime-sum guide shows why the text layer, rune values, and boundaries all matter.

A mathematical metaphor, or something more?

One possible interpretation is that primality suggests an identity that cannot be factored into the possessions, affiliations, and descriptions attached to it. This can be compared with the first koan, where successive descriptions fail to answer who the student is. It is an analogy: people are not integers, and the manuscript does not explicitly equate a possession or affiliation with a factor.

A broader reading treats primality as an original or undivided condition, without requiring a literal arithmetic operation. The known passage does not settle between these interpretations. A useful comparison should explain the actual wording and its neighboring sentences, rather than relying on the word “prime” alone.

Likewise, “divinity within” permits an interpretation involving inward discovery, but does not identify a deity, religious school, or historical source. How the parable changes the emphasis

The earlier section describes a loss. The parable describes a movement: shed circumferences, find divinity within, and emerge. This gives the shared vocabulary a different role. Divinity is not only something the text says can be lost; it is also something its concluding parable asks the reader to discover.
