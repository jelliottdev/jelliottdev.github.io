Dots and punctuation

Compare large dot arrangements, border marks, and small punctuation clusters, with original-image details and page locations.

“Dots” can mean several visibly different things in Liber Primus. Large dots separated by wide spaces, small marks between runes, and compact multi-dot clusters should not be treated as one symbol just because a transcription uses similar punctuation for them.
Five-dot arrangements: where to look
These three pages all contain a group of five large black dots. Their positions relative to the writing differ, so a reference to “the five dots” should include a page ID.
	Page	Location of the dots	Surrounding material
	LP2-07	Below the text	A short red closing line and curled side borders
	LP2-23	Above the text	The opening of the community Mayfly grouping
	LP2-56	Below the text	The An End reading and printed hexadecimal target
This is a location comparison, not a claim that the patterns are identical or encode the same operation. Use each original image for measurements.
LP2-07 has five large black dots beneath its red closing line. They form a loose arrangement rather than one straight row. Smaller specks also appear beside the upper spiral drawings, so “the dots on page 7” should specify which group is meant.

Solid marks and pale rings on LP2-53

The small clusters beside LP2-53’s curled borders differ in fill. The left cluster has three solid black marks and two pale outlined rings; the right has five solid black marks. A count that treats only dark filled shapes as dots would describe these groups differently from a count that includes rings.

These are separately inspected examples, not a verified count across the entire border group. Compare their layouts in the group-opening comparison.
Red closing clusters on LP2-22, LP2-32, and LP2-55
These three pages end their black rune text with a narrow red cluster of small square marks. The clusters occupy a similar place in the text layout even though the drawings below them differ: a reclining figure on LP2-22, a soft-edged tree on LP2-32, and a pale tree above a winged figure on LP2-55.

Calling one a “closing cluster” describes its position. It does not determine whether a key resets there, whether it separates sentences or sections, or whether visually similar clusters represent identical instructions. The color-boundary guide explains which additional rules a cipher proposal must specify. The opening-page component counts below apply to their measured regions; they should not be copied onto these examples without measurement.
Small marks and compact clusters
The LP2-00–02 inventory records punctuation separately from rune shapes. Its measured regions include one-, four-, and thirteen-component groups. A “component” is a connected set of dark pixels under a specified threshold. It is not automatically one intentional dot: touching marks can merge, and tiny specks can be excluded.

The printed mark and the exported character
In the archive notation, a comma represents a three-dot symbol; a period is a clause separator, and an ampersand is a paragraph separator. These are editorial conventions. They are not instructions to insert the prime value of a comma, reset a key, or skip a rune.
One exported character can stand for visually different marks. Before a punctuation-based experiment, identify the exact image regions and the precise sequence you are testing. Otherwise a result may describe the transcriber’s decisions rather than the printed manuscript.
