Spirals and branching borders

Compare curled and branching borders, inspect repeated side-dot regions on all sixteen LP2-40–55 pages, and reproduce the pixel comparison.

Several groups of Liber Primus pages have curled drawings beside the text. The community calls LP2-03–07 “Spirals,” LP2-40–53 “Spiral Branches,” and LP2-54–55 “Spiral & Dead Tree.” These names make the images easier to find. Comparing their actual linework is more useful than treating every curl as the same symbol.

The curled borders on LP2-03–07

On LP2-03, paired curves frame the left and right margins of the runic text. Each side has a tighter upper curl and a longer lower curve. LP2-07 retains this border arrangement, while its lower text ends above a red line and five large black dots.

The dots are a separate feature from the small groups of specks beside the upper curls. See dots and punctuation for examples of why position, size, and arrangement should be recorded separately.

Open the intervening pages: LP2-04 · LP2-05 · LP2-06.

The branching borders on LP2-40–55

LP2-40 has curls joined to branching, root-like forms on both sides of the text. LP2-53 shows a winged insect below its red closing passage. The next pair, LP2-54–55, continues the branching border style; LP2-55 also contains a pale tree-shaped drawing above a winged insect.

Use the illustrated index to inspect the intervening LP2-40–53 pages. The branches and trees article compares these forms with the narrow branching drawing on LP2-08–14.

Compare the group openings: LP2-40 and LP2-54

Both pages begin with an enlarged red rune beside three rows and smaller red text at the start of the first row. On LP2-54, the red opening ends with a compact punctuation cluster before the black runes continue. The shared border style does not erase these differences in text and punctuation.

Each page also has a small cluster beside the inner edge of each border. On the left, three marks are solid black and two are pale ring outlines; on the right, all five are solid. The same fill pattern is recorded on LP2-53, with enlarged views. The opening-text comparison concerns these inspected pages. The separate side-dot audit below covers all sixteen pages from LP2-40 through LP2-55.

Open the LP2-40 observations or LP2-54 observations beside the original images. The pale tree and winged figure that explain the community name “Spiral & Dead Tree” are on LP2-55; the name does not mean both pages contain a tree.

The same side-dot regions on all sixteen pages

Both small side-dot regions are repeated without pixel changes across every page from LP2-40 through LP2-55. We compared each left region with the left region on LP2-40, and each right region with the right region on LP2-40. All 16 images match in both decoded RGB pixels and grayscale pixels, without alignment, resizing or a tolerance.

This is stronger than a visual resemblance, but narrower than a claim that all borders or whole pages are identical. It supports reuse of these fixed image regions. A reused motif could still carry constant information; equality alone does not prove that the marks are merely decoration. This comparison also does not measure reflection between the left and right motifs, or compare the separate five-dot groups on LP2-07, LP2-23 and LP2-56.

A useful comparison starts with a question

QuestionWhat to recordWhat the result would establish
Do two pages reuse the same border?Original-file hashes, crop rectangles, alignment, and pixel differencesAgreement under the stated alignment and tolerance
Are the two margins mirror images?A defined reflection axis and corresponding points on both drawingsHow closely the selected regions match after reflection
Does a curl follow a particular spiral?A centre, sampled points, coordinate scale, fitted parameters, and residualsThe quality of that geometric fit, compared with alternatives

For example, a logarithmic spiral has r = a exp(bθ), while an Archimedean spiral has r = a + bθ. Here r is distance from the chosen centre and θ is angle. Merely seeing a curl does not select either model. The chosen centre and the thickness of the drawn line can strongly affect a fit.

Before connecting geometry to a cipher, state how a measured result would change the decoding operation. “The borders look symmetric” and “read alternate lines backwards” are separate claims; the first does not supply the second without an explicit rule.

Preparing a reproducible image comparison

  1. Choose the pages and the feature before fitting anything. Include a comparison page with a visibly different border.
  2. Download the original files and verify their SHA-256 hashes.
  3. Record rectangles using original-image coordinates. Keep the original pixels and any transformed comparison image separately.
  4. Document scaling, rotation, reflection, thresholding, and the score used to compare the shapes.
  5. Publish the script and output together, including mismatches. A visually plausible overlay alone is not enough to reproduce the comparison.

The completed side-dot comparison above establishes pixel equality within its two rectangles. The broader border-fitting questions remain open, and no cipher role is established.
