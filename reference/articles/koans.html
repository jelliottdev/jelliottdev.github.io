The two koans: identity and the inner voice

A close reading of the master–student exchanges, their use of circumference, and the limits of a Zen attribution.

Liber Primus labels two sections A Koan. Both stage an exchange between a master and a student, but they ask different questions. The first tests the descriptions a person offers as an identity. The second turns a denial of an inner voice into an example of that voice.

Read the first section, LP1-06–09, and the second section, LP1-14–15, alongside this guide. Their pages, literal readings and decoding instructions are separate from the interpretation offered here.

What is a koan?

In Zen contexts, a kōan can take the form of a puzzling question or encounter used in training. It is not simply a brainteaser whose purpose is to produce a clever verbal answer. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account of Japanese Zen, section 2, places kōan practice within cultivation of wisdom and compassion, and describes its particular role in Rinzai training.

That background helps explain why a master might reject an answer that is factually correct. It does not identify Liber Primus’s dialogues with any particular traditional case. The manuscript gives the genre label but supplies no author, collection, case number or lineage for either story.

First koan: what an answer describes

The first student seeks admission to study. Each answer to the master’s identity question changes the kind of description being offered:

Answer offeredHow the master classifies itWhat changes in the exchange
A nameA label used to address the personThe question is separated from naming.
A professionAn activity or social roleThe question is separated from work.
Being humanMembership in a speciesThe answer moves from social identity to a biological category.
Consciousness inhabiting a bodyAn account of what the person isEven an abstract account does not satisfy the master’s distinction.

The student eventually begins an answer and cannot complete it. After the pause, the master admits him. The story ends with admission rather than a final definition of the self. One reading is that the student has reached the limits of the descriptions he initially relied on. Another emphasis would be his change from confident answers to uncertainty. Neither reading requires supplying a missing final word as if it were decoded plaintext.

The instruction that follows the exchange is a separate textual feature. Its comparison with Lewis Carroll is discussed in the Carroll guide; a possible connection there does not identify the source of the preceding dialogue.

Second koan: the denial becomes the example

The second master associates the “I” with the voice of the circumference. A student inwardly denies having such a voice and prepares to object. The master points to the just-formed denial as the example the student was looking for.

The exchange is self-referential: an act of thinking about thought becomes part of the argument. Its effect depends on the narration telling us what this student thought. The story does not establish that every person experiences thought as verbal inner speech, nor does it offer a psychological test of readers.

Its vocabulary also belongs to the surrounding manuscript. The Loss of Divinity assigns consumption, preservation and adherence to the circumference. LP2-57’s parable describes shedding circumferences. Following those uses gives the term more context than treating it immediately as a reference to circles in a diagram.

How the two stories work together

The first dialogue unsettles what the student can say about himself; the second questions the voice doing the saying. This is a useful internal connection between the stories. It also helps frame the Welcome section’s promised end to self: what is being brought to an end—a person, a description, or a habitual way of identifying oneself? The manuscript’s stories make that question available without settling it through a formal definition.

Compare Emerson’s self-trust with care. The earlier puzzle’s use of Emerson is documented, but “self” need not mean the same thing in every work. Likewise, identifying the stories as koans does not establish that every claim in Liber Primus belongs to Zen doctrine.

What would establish an outside source?

A source claim needs a specific passage, a traceable edition or translation, and enough shared wording or structure to compare the exchanges in detail. A generic master-and-student story is weak evidence by itself. If a candidate is found, record differences as well as similarities and ask whether the source predates the manuscript.

Keep a literary interpretation separate from a cipher proposal. If “circumference” suggests a key or a reading path, state the exact symbols and transformation, then follow the reproduction requirements. A thematic connection is a reason to investigate; the resulting computation still needs to be checked.
