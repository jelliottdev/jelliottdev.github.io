Euler’s totient and prime-minus-one

Why φ(p) equals p − 1 for primes, and what that means for LP2-56.

Euler’s totient function, written φ(n), counts the integers from 1 through n that are relatively prime to n. Two integers are relatively prime when their greatest common divisor is 1. “Relatively prime” does not mean that both integers are themselves prime.

Small examples
For n = 8, the qualifying integers are 1, 3, 5, and 7, so φ(8) = 4. For a prime p, every integer from 1 through p − 1 is relatively prime to p. Therefore φ(p) = p − 1. In particular, φ(2), φ(3), φ(5), φ(7) are 1, 2, 4, 6.

	n	Integers counted	φ(n)	n − 1

	8	1, 3, 5, 7	4	7

	9	1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8	6	8

	11	1 through 10	10	10


The 9 row demonstrates the meaning of “relatively prime”: 4 and 8 are composite, but both are counted because neither shares a divisor greater than one with 9. The numbers 3, 6, and 9 are excluded because they share the factor 3. The function counts a relationship to n, not how many primes occur below n.

For prime p, the only positive divisors are 1 and p. An integer k between 1 and p − 1 cannot share the divisor p, so its greatest common divisor with p must be 1. There are exactly p − 1 such integers. This proves the identity used by the LP2-56 method without needing a table of precomputed totients.

Why the LP2-56 method has two names
The archived walkthrough describes LP2-56 using “phi(prime).” The prime-minus-one guide uses the equivalent expression p − 1. These are the same shifts when the input sequence consists of primes. Substituting φ(n) for consecutive integers n would produce a different sequence.

	Sequence used as input	Inputs	Totients

	Consecutive primes	2, 3, 5, 7, 11	1, 2, 4, 6, 10

	Consecutive integers starting at 2	2, 3, 4, 5, 6	1, 2, 2, 4, 2


The first two shifts agree, but the third already differs. “Use totients” therefore leaves an essential question unanswered: totients of which input sequence? It also differs from the prime-counting function, which counts how many primes are at most a given number.

How to reproduce the identity
from math import gcd
def phi(n):
    return sum(gcd(k, n) == 1 for k in range(1, n + 1))
assert phi(8) == 4
assert [phi(p) for p in [2, 3, 5, 7]] == [1, 2, 4, 6]
This deliberately simple implementation is useful for small examples. Reproduce the actual page with python scripts/reproduce_reading.py lp2-56, including its recorded interruption and modulo-29 arithmetic.

What the identity does not determine
It does not specify the starting prime, direction, reset behavior, or copied rune positions. Those parameters remain part of the keystream convention.
