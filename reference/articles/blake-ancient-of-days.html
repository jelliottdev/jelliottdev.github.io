The Ancient of Days: the lower compasses panel

The frontispiece, the figure with compasses, and why the exact Blake copy matters.

The lower central panel of the compasses cover is identified as a detail of William Blake’s The Ancient of Days by Frank G. Bosman’s cover study. The complete composition supplies context that is lost when the hand and instrument are isolated.

What is a frontispiece?

A frontispiece is an illustration placed at the beginning of a book. The British Museum’s record for Europe: a Prophecy, copy A, plate 1 identifies this design as its frontispiece. The catalogue describes a bearded figure in a luminous sphere, extending compasses into the darkness below. It gives the figure’s identity as probably Urizen; that qualification should remain when citing the record.

The collection record describes a particular copy. A work title alone does not identify which impression, colouring or modern reproduction was used in the puzzle image. This entry does not establish that the collage was made from the museum’s copy A.

How to compare the cover

  1. Open the original cover and the museum’s complete design.
  2. Trace the hand and the two legs of the instrument. Compare their relationship to the surrounding light and the partly visible head.
  3. Check the orientation: Bosman describes a rotated fragment. Record the rotation and crop separately instead of treating a transformed image as a different drawing.
  4. To identify an exact source copy, compare small distinctive details and colour placement across documented copies, not merely the common outline.

What the repeated instrument tells us

Newton, in the upper panel, also supplies a hand with compasses. The collage therefore places two acts of measurement together. Reading that arrangement as a theme is different from extracting a key from it: a cipher proposal needs an explicit rule that someone else can apply to the same source bytes.

See Nebuchadnezzar for the side panels and the proposed Daniel connection, and William Blake and Liber Primus for the manuscript’s textual allusion.
