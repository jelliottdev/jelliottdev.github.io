Newton: the upper compasses panel

The drawing instrument, Blake’s portrayal of Newton, and how to compare the cover fragment.

The upper central panel of the illustrated cover concentrates on a hand, compasses and a geometrical drawing. Frank G. Bosman’s published identification traces this fragment to William Blake’s Newton. Understanding the whole composition helps explain what the crop leaves out.

[Image: Upper central panel of the preserved cover, showing a hand holding compasses over a geometrical drawing]
Upper central panel, shown as a cropped view of the preserved cover. The hand, open compasses and drawing are the features to compare with Newton. The view retains the collage’s orientation; no reflection has been applied. Open the complete original JPEG.

What the original depicts

Tate’s reproduction and description show Newton concentrating on a drawing while seated on a textured rock. Tate interprets his attention to the instrument and scroll, and his disregard for his surroundings, as Blake’s criticism of a view of the world confined to rational measurement. This is the museum’s account of Blake’s image, not an explanation supplied by the puzzle authors.

Here, compasses means the hinged drawing instrument used to construct circles and transfer distances. It is not a magnetic compass for finding north. That distinction matters before proposing an operation based on direction, angle or radius.

Identify the museum object

Tate’s published metadata identifies its Newton as N05058. An accession number identifies an object in a collection; it is not a date or a manuscript page number. The record gives the following details:

Date
1795–c. 1805; the “c.” means approximately.
Medium
Colour print, ink and watercolour on paper.
Support dimensions
460 × 600 mm, as recorded. These are physical dimensions, not pixel dimensions of an online image.
Acquisition credit
Presented by W. Graham Robertson, 1939.

Download the unmodified Tate metadata record, or inspect its pinned source through the source and checksum manifest. Tate labels this repository as an October 2014 dataset that is no longer maintained; it is a dated collection record, not a claim about the museum’s current display or latest cataloguing. Tate dataset documentation.

The accession identifies Tate’s object. It does not establish that this particular impression, or Tate’s digital photograph of it, supplied the puzzle fragment. Keep that question separate from recognizing the composition.

How to check the fragment

Open the preserved cover JPEG and Tate’s image in separate tabs.
Locate the hand, the two legs of the compasses and the drawing beneath them. Compare their arrangement, rather than matching only the general subject.
Bosman describes the Newton fragment as mirrored. Check left and right against the complete work; record any reflection separately from cropping.
Keep the original JPEG unchanged. If you make an annotated comparison, label it as a derivative and record the source image used.

From artwork to a research question

The other central panel also contains compasses: see The Ancient of Days. Their repetition makes measurement a reasonable subject for interpretation. It does not specify a numerical cipher. A test based on the drawing would still need to say which points are measured, how image scaling is handled, and how a measurement becomes a symbol.

For the manuscript’s own language about perception and the individual, follow philosophical themes. The Blake overview treats the separate verbal parallel in Welcome.
