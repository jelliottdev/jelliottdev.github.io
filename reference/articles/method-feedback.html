Autokey and ciphertext feedback

Trace seeds, state updates and interruptions before testing a feedback decoding rule.

A feedback cipher uses something already seen to choose the next key value. That “something” might be an earlier ciphertext rune, a rune you just decoded, or a running sum. These rules can produce different readings from the same input. “Autokey” alone does not tell us which rule to use.

What the key remembers

Here is one invented example using the site's 29-rune alphabet. The ciphertext is I–L–C, with indices 10, 20, 5. Begin with key O, index 3. At each step subtract the current key modulo 29. The first output is 10 − 3 = 7, or W, in every rule.

Previous ciphertext
Use the last input as the next key. The next outputs are 20 − 10 = 10 (I) and 5 − 20 ≡ 14 (X). Reading: W–I–X.
Previous plaintext
Use the last decoded rune instead. The next outputs are 20 − 7 = 13 (P) and 5 − 13 ≡ 21 (NG). Reading: W–P–NG.
Running plaintext prime sum
After W, add its GP prime value, 19, to the key: 3 + 19 = 22. The next output is 20 − 22 ≡ 27 (IA). Add IA's prime value, 107, so the key becomes 22 + 107 ≡ 13; the last output is 5 − 13 ≡ 21 (NG). Reading: W–IA–NG.

These are demonstrations, not manuscript readings. The third rule uses prime values, whereas the first two use rune indices. A longer seed, a different sign, or a reset at a word break changes the rule. A fixed known-text running key does not depend on newly recovered plaintext and is a separate idea.

The feedback opening test found none of nine fixed 12-rune openings under its scalar-seed rules. The prime-valued plaintext test tried two prime-value update rules, including the running-sum example above, on complete LP2 pages; 144 of 199 matched shuffled searches scored at least as high as its best output. The seed-family test covers other explicit plaintext and ciphertext rules. Each finding applies to its written equation and bounds.

The weighted ciphertext-feedback test and its remaining-page extension cover all 55 rune-bearing LP2 pages under specified one-to-three-rune history rules and constant seeds 0 or 1. No output clears the source’s English-score bar. The coefficient choices are bounded, and constant-seed controls do not establish recovery when a varying key skips positions.

The multi-rune history replay shows why a known-seed demonstration is not enough: its restricted seed search misses the source’s planted plaintext-history and mixed-history messages, even though supplying the correct seed recovers them. That search cannot exclude those broader feedback models.

A changing alphabet is another way to store state. See moving disks, rotors and evolving alphabets for a worked example.

An interruption needs a complete state rule

“Skip F” can mean omitting it, copying it without updating state, copying it while updating state, or resetting the state before the next symbol. Those choices may agree at F and disagree everywhere afterward. Specify the output, state update and counter update separately. Also declare whether headings, spaces and page breaks affect the state.

The LP2-54–55 case study works through a particular previous-ciphertext rule with six candidate interruption positions. Its reset and skip records test different state policies. Despite “autokey” in their record identifiers, the documented state comes from ciphertext. These records do not test every plaintext-autokey construction.

Define a finite search

An unrestricted L-rune seed has 29^L possibilities. A word list gives a smaller, explicitly different search. For m fixed candidate interruptions, independently selecting or rejecting each gives 2^m subsets. Searching seed length, feedback source, lag, sign and interruption policies adds further choices. Count the combinations actually tried and deduplicate identical outputs.

A particular ciphertext running-sum proposal predicts that repeated output runes correspond to plaintext EA when its key is nonzero. The equation and proof state that condition; the solved-text control checks the required EA frequency. This tests that proposal, not every feedback cipher.

Check the state trace, then the reading

Save the source position, input, state before decoding, output and state afterward. For an intended reversible cipher, verify that the corresponding encoder recovers the full input. A round trip checks the implementation; it does not prove that the cipher was used in Liber Primus.

Choose the rule on one segment and apply it unchanged to a continuation. Any seed, reset or correction added after inspecting that continuation becomes another fitted choice. Compare the complete search with controls, including its selection of the best-looking output. See null models, differential decoding and all method families.
