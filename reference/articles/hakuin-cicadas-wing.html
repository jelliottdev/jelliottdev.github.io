Hakuin’s cicada: emergence and the limits of help

Trace The Cicada’s Wing to its printed English attribution, compare it with LP2-57, and distinguish a literary parallel from a source or cipher claim.

The Cicada’s Wing is a title used online for a short passage attributed to Hakuin Ekaku. It offers a specific comparison with Liber Primus: an insect’s emergence becomes an analogy for human development. The comparison is worth reading, but it does not establish Hakuin as a source for the manuscript or identify the detached wing on LP2-27.

What happens in the passage?

A narrator watches a cicada struggling to shed its old skin. He tries to free a trapped wing, but the wing then fails to open. He regrets intervening and compares his mistake with teachers who damage students’ development through ill-timed guidance. The point concerns the consequences of teaching, not just the insect’s transformation. Read the online excerpt.

Which text is this?

The passage appears as an unnumbered opening epigraph in Norman Waddell’s translation, The Essential Teachings of Zen Master Hakuin (Shambhala, 1994; ISBN 0-87773-972-2), before the contents. The printed attribution names a letter to Layman Kokan. The title page identifies the main translated work as Sokkō-roku Kaien-fusetsu. An epigraph is a short quotation placed before a book or section; its location does not make it a numbered chapter of that work. See the publisher’s listing for a later edition.

The online excerpt gives “Koan” instead of Kokan and dates Hakuin to 1786. The inspected 1994 edition and the Metropolitan Museum’s Hakuin record give 1686–1769. These discrepancies matter when tracing a quotation.

The opening epigraph was checked against the scanned title, copyright and attribution pages. This establishes a printed English witness before Liber Primus. It does not establish the original letter’s date, Japanese wording, or the history of the online title. A comparison based on exact English words must name the translation.

Compare the stories, not just the insect

LP2-57’s complete reading compares an instar’s movement to the surface with shedding circumferences and finding divinity within. That is a different narrative from a teacher intervening in another creature’s emergence. The shared image is the starting point of the comparison; the differences help define its limits.

This comparison can sharpen a reading without proving influence. Ask whether the manuscript emphasizes a person’s own work, another person’s guidance, or both. Then return to its actual sentences: the Welcome, the two master–student dialogues, and the recurring uses of circumference. A proposed outside parallel should not replace those internal connections.

Two manuscript images to keep separate

The first image belongs to the Wing & Tree section; the second accompanies the final parable. A wing in both discussions is not enough to connect their image sources. The Hakuin passage also does not justify labeling the LP2-27 drawing as the left wing described in the story. Species, orientation and source attribution each require their own evidence.

For a closer view of the final figure, use the original-image detail in the instar guide. That guide distinguishes the biological term from the manuscript’s analogy.

Is this one of the manuscript’s koans?

Not on the evidence presented here. The printed attribution identifies a letter excerpt. Liber Primus explicitly titles two different dialogues A Koan, but does not name Hakuin or this letter in their known readings. “Kokan,” the recipient’s name in the printed attribution, should not be confused with the genre word kōan.

The Zen background guide explains why teaching context matters. Calling every story with a spiritual lesson a koan would erase distinctions between letters, parables, teaching dialogues and particular practice traditions.

From a literary lead to a test

A source claim needs a specific correspondence: shared phrasing, a distinctive narrative sequence, or an image match with identifiable features. A cipher proposal needs more: a fixed edition, selected passage, conversion into runes, key rule, target pages and success criterion. Without those choices, saying that a book was “tried” leaves the search impossible to reproduce.

No Hakuin-key experiment is recorded by this article. It is a reading comparison with a checked English source, linked through works and influences. A future bounded test belongs in Have we tried it?, with its inputs and results separate from this interpretation.
