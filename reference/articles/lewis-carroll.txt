Four unreasonable things and Lewis Carroll

LP1-09 compared with the White Queen: doing versus believing, four versus six, and the limits of the association.

The instruction at the end of Liber Primus's first koan can be compared with the White Queen's impossible-things speech in Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass. The comparison concerns a numbered practice that challenges ordinary expectations. It is a possible literary association, not an established quotation or source attribution.

Compare the actual words

	Source	Wording

	LP1-09, contextual English	DO FOUR UNREASONABLE THINGS EACH DAY

	Carroll, chapter V, “Wool and Water”	Why, sometimes I've believed as many as six impossible things before breakfast.


Carroll's sentence is spoken by the White Queen in Through the Looking-Glass. The primary text at Project Gutenberg places it in a conversation about believing something Alice considers impossible. The Queen treats belief as something improved by practice.

The manuscript's literal sequence, after joining its line wrap, is DO-FOUR-UNREASONABLE-THNGS-EACH-DAY. THINGS is contextual English for THNGS under the rune-label convention. Do not silently replace the literal sequence when investigating its numbers. See transliteration and the complete first koan.

The differences matter

Action: the manuscript says to do things; the Queen describes believing things.
Number: the manuscript gives four; Carroll gives as many as six.
Qualifier: unreasonable is not identical to impossible. An action can be considered unreasonable without being logically or physically impossible.
Time: the manuscript says each day; Carroll's example is before breakfast.
Voice: the manuscript presents a direct instruction, while Carroll gives a character's recollection within a comic dialogue.

Those differences prevent calling the manuscript line a verbatim Carroll quotation. The structural resemblance is worth recording, but it is less specific than the holy-life verbal parallel with Blake. This article does not establish that Carroll was the immediate source of the instruction.

Read the instruction after the story

In LP1-06–09, the master rejects the student's name, occupation, species and description of consciousness as answers to who the student is. Admission to study follows the student's inability to complete another answer. The four-things instruction comes after this exchange, separated as an instruction. A possible reading is that it extends the story's challenge to familiar explanations into a daily exercise. That is editorial interpretation, not an additional decoded message.

Carroll's scene instead concerns Alice's refusal to believe an apparently impossible age and the Queen's response. The two contexts both challenge ordinary judgments, but their immediate questions differ: identity in the koan, belief in the Carroll exchange. A similarity in tone does not demonstrate identical philosophical commitments.

What happens around the White Queen’s remark?

The sentence appears within a longer sequence of reversals in chapter V of the primary text. Reading that sequence helps distinguish a character’s comic argument from a direct instruction by the author.

	Moment in the scene	What the dialogue does

	Memory and punishment	The Queen remembers future events and describes a messenger punished before his trial and crime. Alice challenges the order.

	The brooch	The Queen screams before pricking her finger. After the injury, she explains that the screaming is already finished.

	Age and belief	To distract Alice from loneliness, the Queen turns to age. Alice rejects the Queen’s claimed age of 101 years, five months and a day. The Queen responds by presenting belief as something to practise.


The impossible-things remark belongs to this last exchange. The scene repeatedly unsettles ordinary expectations about sequence and explanation. This is a reading of the scene’s structure, not evidence that the manuscript adopts the Queen’s reasoning.

How this changes the comparison with Liber Primus

In Carroll, Alice continues to question the speaker: the dialogue lets a reader notice the gap in an argument. In the first koan, the student’s difficulty answering leads to admission, and the subsequent instruction asks for action. Reading either passage as a simple endorsement of believing anything would erase those differences.

For the manuscript’s own treatment of belief, compare testing knowledge with the opening warning. The instruction to do something unreasonable and the instruction to test claims can be read together as a tension worth examining. Calling that tension intentional, or resolving it through Carroll, would require further evidence.

The Queen’s reversed sequence also does not establish a rune-reversal operation. A literary motif becomes a cipher proposal only after the input and transformation are specified below.

What would make this a cipher hypothesis?

A comparison becomes a testable cipher proposal only when it specifies an operation. “Use Carroll” leaves the text, edition, passage and transformation undetermined. A proposal would need to fix those choices, declare how punctuation and letters become rune indices, and state which manuscript positions consume the key. Record those decisions before inspecting candidate outputs.

The difference between four and six does not itself justify subtracting two from every rune, choosing a six-character key, or treating chapter V as an offset. Each would be a new assumption requiring its own scoped test. Neither the comparison here nor a search result in the wiki counts as evidence that such a technique has been tried.
