Consumption, preservation, and adherence

Read the three behaviors in The Loss of Divinity alongside its advice about wealth, attachment, reason and belonging.

The Loss of Divinity names three behaviors of the circumference: consumption, preservation and adherence. The passage does more than list undesirable actions. It gives motives for them, connects them to a loss of primality, and then follows them with advice about wealth and attachment. Reading those parts together helps explain why its argument is not simply “own nothing” or “reject every belief.”

The source is the complete reading of LP1-10–13. The explanations below paraphrase and interpret that section; they are not additional decoded text or instructions from Liberpedia.

The three behaviors and their stated motives

	Behavior	Reason given in the passage	What the criticism concerns

	Consumption	A belief that there is not enough, or that present resources came through luck and cannot be obtained again.	Consuming too much in response to perceived shortage or future inability.

	Preservation	A belief that losing things would leave a person unable to regain them.	Keeping things because of an assumed weakness or dependence.

	Adherence	Following dogma or reason to belong and to be right.	The purpose for following an idea, including the desire for group membership and vindication.


The first two descriptions share a concern about future capability: will the person be able to obtain what they need again? The third changes the object of dependence from things to belonging and correctness. This is an internal structural comparison. It does not establish a psychological diagnosis or an outside philosophical source.

Consumption: quantity matters

The passage criticizes consuming too much and says that most things are not worth consuming. Those qualifications matter. Replacing them with a claim that all consumption is wrong would change the argument. Its focus is the connection it proposes between excess and the beliefs it calls errors within a deception.

Nor does the text establish that scarcity is always imaginary. It presents a view of the circumference’s motivations; it supplies no empirical survey of scarcity, need or human behavior. A close reading can describe that view without accepting it as a universal fact.

Preservation: what is being kept, and why?

Preservation repeats the concern about being unable to recover a loss. The later advice about possessions makes attachment a useful lens for reading this concern. A thing may be retained because it is useful, or because losing it threatens the identity or security built around it. The passage supports examining that distinction, although it does not provide a practical test for separating the two.

There is an apparent tension with the opening warning, which demands that the book’s words and numbers remain unchanged. One possible reconciliation distinguishes preserving a source accurately from holding possessions out of fear. Another reading may leave the tension unresolved. Neither interpretation authorizes silently changing the warning or erasing the later criticism. The knowledge and authority guide discusses preserving evidence while questioning its claims.

Adherence: why both dogma and reason appear

The two alternatives share the same stated purpose: belonging and being right. Removing reason from the comparison would make the passage sound like a criticism only of dogmatic belief. Removing the purpose would make it sound like a rejection of reasoning itself. Both changes would lose part of the recorded argument.

The following claim about belonging and death is stronger than the preceding account of motives. In the wider manuscript, death and the end of self recur alongside emergence. That makes a metaphorical reading available, but does not prove a single intended meaning. It is especially important to distinguish what this text asserts from what the wiki can independently establish.

Why the advice about wealth matters

After connecting the three behaviors to a loss of primality and divinity, the section permits accumulating great wealth while rejecting attachment to what is owned. Its further instruction about readiness to destroy possessions intensifies that contrast. Possession and dependence are therefore not treated as identical.

That sequence complicates both a simple celebration of wealth and a simple demand for poverty. The text does not explain how to judge readiness, what counts as attachment, or whether its severe language is literal or rhetorical. Those remain questions for interpretation, not missing details to supply as established facts.
