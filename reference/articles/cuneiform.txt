Cuneiform-like mark

Compare the repeated mark on LP2-33–39, including its different placement on the first page.

A compact, two-row mark repeats on LP2-33–39. It is commonly called the cuneiform mark. The visible mark belongs in an illustrated index even though its intended meaning is not established.LP2-33: the mark appears below the runic text, near the foot of the page. Where to find it
Placement matters. On LP2-33 the mark is at the bottom; on LP2-34–39 it is above the main text. Calling it a “header” without that exception would hide a potentially relevant layout difference. What the name tells us
The British Museum’s Mesopotamia guide explains how early pictograms developed into wedge-shaped writing called cuneiform. Here it is the community’s name for a visual resemblance. Identifying the manuscript mark as a particular historical sign would require a sign-by-sign comparison with a specified script, period, orientation, and reading. The label alone is not a translation.
The repeated motif is also not part of the ordinary 29-rune Gematria Primus alphabet. Do not assign it a rune index or prime value merely because it occurs beside runic text.
Other details in this section
LP2-33 and LP2-39 show conspicuous separated black dots. LP2-36–38 include red decimal labels: 1 on page 36, 2–4 on page 37, and 5 on page 38. These observations help locate parts of the section; they do not establish what a dot or label instructs the reader to do. See dots and punctuation for the distinction between printed shapes and transcription notation.
How to compare repeated marks
Download the original JPEGs from the page records.
Record a rectangle around each mark in original-image pixels. Include enough surrounding whitespace to preserve orientation.
Compare both appearance and placement. Scaling a preview for display is not evidence that the source marks have identical dimensions.
If testing pixel equality, state the alignment, scale, threshold, and compression tolerance. Visual similarity alone does not establish identical bytes.
The image-region guide explains coordinate and threshold conventions. This article records location and appearance; it does not claim a completed pixel-equality test or a decipherment.
