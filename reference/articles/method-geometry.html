Geometric constructions and navigation

Connect measured landmarks to explicit constructions, tolerances and decoding rules.

A geometric proposal turns locations, distances, angles or paths into a decoding operation. Examples include compass constructions, reflections, folds, nearest-neighbor routes and coordinate lookups. The essential step is explaining how a measured feature determines a symbol or an operation. Recognizing an interesting shape alone does not supply that rule.

Each stage should have a saved output so another person can locate the first disagreement. Annotated overlays are especially useful: show the original landmarks, rejected alternatives and tolerances, rather than only the final successful path.

Coordinates need a declared frame

A point measured at pixel coordinates (x, y) depends on the image size and crop. Normalizing x and y separately by width and height makes coordinates comparable across proportional resizing, but changes distance and angle calculations if the axes receive different scale factors. State whether geometry uses original pixel units, a common scale, or a deliberately normalized rectangle.

For a rotation about a chosen center, record the angle and direction; for reflection, record the axis. A transformation of positions becomes a substitution only after labels and a selection rule are attached. The compass guide explains why a half-turn of 29 equally spaced labels falls between labels and does not automatically implement Atbash.

Convert a preview measurement back to the source

Suppose a crop begins at source position (50, 100) and measures 400 × 600 source pixels. It is displayed at 200 × 300 pixels. A point 25 pixels from the displayed crop's left edge and 50 from its top edge corresponds to:

x_source = 50 + 25 × (400 / 200) = 100
y_source = 100 + 50 × (600 / 300) = 200

The display coordinates are relative to the image crop, not the browser window. Borders, page margins and scrolling do not belong in the source-coordinate calculation. This example assumes the crop is displayed without further trimming, rotation or stretching. Keep crop origin, source dimensions and displayed image dimensions with the measurement.

Different horizontal and vertical scale factors distort geometry. A source displacement of (3, 4) has Euclidean length 5. Doubling both coordinates gives (6, 8), length 10, preserving its direction. Doubling only the horizontal coordinate gives (6, 4), length √52 ≈ 7.21, and changes the direction. A circle under that nonuniform scaling becomes an ellipse.

Points used to fit a transformation do not independently validate it

A translation has two freely chosen offsets. One matched point supplies both: to send (10, 20) to (13, 25), choose (dx, dy) = (3, 5). The exact agreement at that point follows from how the offsets were selected.

Freeze that translation and test another landmark. It predicts that (30, 40) maps to (33, 45). A separately measured target at (34, 45) has a residual distance of one pixel from that prediction. Whether this is acceptable depends on a declared tolerance and measurement uncertainty, not on whether the selected rune looks useful.

Keep fitting landmarks separate from evaluation landmarks, and distinguish an independently identified correspondence from a point chosen because it falls near the transformed position. Allowing rotation, scale, stretching or local warping adds flexibility. Report every allowed transformation and the fitting procedure before interpreting a close overlay as evidence.

A larger radius changes which symbols qualify

For an invented predicted point at (0, 0) and candidate rune centers at (2, 0) and (4, 0), define a hit as distance ≤ r:

Radius rQualifying centersRequired outcome
1NoneRecord a miss.
2Only (2, 0)One geometrically eligible symbol.
4Both centersApply the declared multiple-hit rule.

Choosing the nearest center is a different rule from accepting every center inside a radius. Without a maximum distance, nearest-center selection will return something whenever candidates exist, even if the construction misses them all by a large margin. Preserve distances, misses and ties alongside the output symbols.

Tolerance is a parameter, not a repair tool

If a construction selects every center within radius r of a predicted point, choose r before examining the resulting text. Define what happens with zero hits or multiple hits. Increasing r until a desired rune is selected changes the experiment. Small landmark perturbations can reveal whether a result is stable or relies on a particular pixel placement; report the perturbation range and all resulting changes.

Existing tests and useful extensions

The shared-radii experiment tests its declared landmark and distance construction. The totient-map route experiment evaluates a different proposed coordinate route with its own choices. Neither result covers all geometric constructions.

A new test should fix its landmarks, allowed transformations and scoring rule, then apply the same procedure to appropriate controls. Depending on the claim, controls might shuffle labels while preserving the geometry, or perturb positions while preserving labels. These ask different questions and must not be substituted silently. If many paths or tolerances compete, controls must repeat that search too.

Repeated artwork also matters: an identical border on several pages is not several independent examples of a construction. Inspect the margin-reuse evidence before counting repetitions. For discrete grid paths, see route methods; for image transformations without a geometric decoding rule, see image processing.
