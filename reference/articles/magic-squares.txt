Magic squares in the 2014 puzzle

Convert LP1-05 rune words into the 1033 square, check LP1-16’s 3301 square, and distinguish both from the embedded grids and LP2-15 panel.

A magic square has equal sums in every row, every column, and both main diagonals. Its order is its width and height; its magic constant is that common sum. Equal row sums alone are not enough.

The 1033 square has two preserved forms: the mixed number-and-rune grid printed on LP1-05 and the numerical matrix extracted from that page’s original JPEG. The latter can be replayed from an archived server capture.

Which number grid are you looking for?
	Location	What is present	What is checked
	LP1-05	Five-by-five grid of numbers and rune words	After rune-value substitution, all twelve lines sum to 1033
	LP1-16	Five-by-five grid of printed numbers	All twelve lines sum to 3301 directly
	LP1-10–13 embedded messages	ASCII grids with differing borders	The displayed three-by-three interior alone is not magic
	LP2-15	Four-by-four decimal panel, with red 3299	Printed rows and columns have unequal sums
	LP2-49–51	Two-character letter-and-number entries between plant borders	256 entries in eight-column arrays; no numeral base assumed
These are different objects. Specify the page, whether the grid is visible or extracted, and any substitution before comparing totals.
Magic does not always mean normal
A normal order-n magic square uses each integer from 1 to n² exactly once. A general magic square need not use that range or distinct entries. Keep the definition separate from any additional rules in a historical challenge.

LP1-05: turning rune words into a 1033 square
The Some Wisdom page, LP1-05, prints a five-by-five grid containing both decimal numbers and rune words below CNOW THIS. The all-number version recorded in the historical walkthrough is a conversion of that mixed grid. Keep each printed number unchanged; replace each rune word with the sum of its Gematria Primus prime values.

Equal sums do not mean matching words
Turn the converted LP1-05 number grid through 180 degrees and every value matches its opposite. Do the same with the printed tokens and five of the twelve opposite pairs differ. The word pairs SHADOWS/CABAL, BUFFERS/MOURNFUL and CARNAL/ANALOG have equal additive rune values despite having different rune sequences. The other two pairs put a decimal number opposite a rune word. Thus the symmetry belongs to the converted values, not to identical writing in every opposite cell.
This is an example of a sum collision: different inputs produce the same total. Addition also loses order, so rearranging the same runes leaves their sum unchanged. A total therefore cannot uniquely recover a word, establish that two words mean the same thing, or specify a decoding rule. The spelling and prime-sum guide explains why word order and rune choice must be kept separate when evaluating unusual spellings.
To reproduce this distinction, run python scripts/check_number_examples.py and inspect lp1_05.half_turn_pairs. Each pair records its positions, Latin labels, whether its original tokens match, and its converted values. The output reports printed_tokens_half_turn_symmetric: false and converted_values_half_turn_symmetric: true. The first comparison is between stored tokens, not a pixel-by-pixel test of the handwriting.
The printed 3301 square on LP1-16

The final LP1 sheet contains a five-by-five decimal square below the heading read as CNOW THIS. These are printed numbers, not prime values calculated from runes. The complete instruction reading preserves their position after the text and insect illustration.

[Image: LP1-16 detail: the red heading above five rows of decimal numbers.]
Original-image detail, rectangle [500, 1800, 1400, 900]. Complete page · Original JPEG.

 434 1311 312  278  966
 204  812 934  280 1071
 626  620 809  620  626
1071  280 934  812  204
 966  278 312 1311  434

Every row, every column and both main diagonals sum to 3301. For example, the first row gives 434 + 1311 + 312 + 278 + 966 = 3301; the descending diagonal gives 434 + 812 + 809 + 812 + 434 = 3301. The total of all 25 entries is 16505, which is five times the common line sum. The square is magic under the definition above, but not normal: it repeats values and does not use 1 through 25.

It also has half-turn symmetry: rotate the grid through 180 degrees and every entry returns to an equal entry. In zero-based coordinates, A[r][c] = A[4-r][4-c]. The center, 809, stays in place. This is different from reflecting only left-to-right: the first row reversed is the last row, not the first row. Symmetry and equal sums are separate properties, so both should be checked.

Can the numbers be replaced with words?
The candidate-word experiment checks sixteen supplied words against their proposed totals and preserves all 24 rune-label segmentations. Every proposed total is achievable, but THAN and AETHEREAL admit alternative segmentations with different sums. Replacing a cell with an equal-sum word preserves the square’s arithmetic automatically; it does not recover a unique word or prove a hidden reading.

A related five-candidate variant checks PROVE WORTHIER FIND TRUTHS and MYRMELEONTIDAE. It retains ten segmentations and shows why the proposed 809 match requires EO as one rune but A–E as two. Both records belong to the same word-sum family.

Arithmetic fit is only the first question. A pinned-corpus control finds 38, 15, 10 and 38 words at those four totals and rune lengths: 216,600 ordered combinations before grammar or meaning is considered. The corpus is a surviving version of a linked crib-assist tool, not a verified copy of the historical worddata.csv. These are possible slot fillings, not 216,600 readable sentences or evidence that a particular phrase was intended.

Does one chosen prime determine the square?
No, under the fixed-line-sum and half-turn rules alone. Our constraint experiment finds six rational degrees of freedom for an order-five square; fixing any one entry leaves five. It also supplies a different positive-integer square for every chosen cell in both LP1 examples. The worked 809 example changes only four entries while preserving all twelve totals.
Counting equations is not enough: some repeat constraints already implied by others. The experiment distinguishes half-turn symmetry from transpose symmetry and explains why a rational dimension is not a count of positive-integer solutions. Additional prime-pattern or allowed-value requirements would be separate constraints, not part of this result.
The later LP1 payloads
LP1-10 through LP1-13 contain signed instructions referring to three magic squares. Their ASCII borders differ. If one removes the surrounding border digits, the displayed interior is:
10 12 10
12 14 12
10 12 10
Its row sums are 32, 38, and 32. It is not itself a magic square under the definition above. Do not label the visible number arrangement as a solved magic square merely because the surrounding prose mentions them.
The historical walkthrough later describes an input form accepting squares subject to criteria, with an order-5 square summing to 3301 as one example. This wiki has not independently reconstructed the historical server validator. That report does not prove that the earlier 1033 square was the required submission.

The LP2-15 decimal panel is another distinct arrangement; its printed row and column sums are unequal.
How to check a square

This complete Python 3 example uses the historical arrangement above. It prints row, column, and diagonal totals separately, so a failed check can be located rather than hidden behind a single yes/no result. It needs no files or extra packages.

square = [
    [272, 138, 341, 131, 151],
    [366, 199, 130, 320, 18],
    [226, 245, 91, 245, 226],
    [18, 320, 130, 199, 366],
    [151, 131, 341, 138, 272],
]
n = len(square)
assert n and all(len(row) == n for row in square)
rows = [sum(row) for row in square]
columns = [sum(row[c] for row in square) for c in range(n)]
diagonals = [sum(square[i][i] for i in range(n)),
             sum(square[i][n - 1 - i] for i in range(n))]
lines = rows + columns + diagonals
is_magic = len(set(lines)) == 1
entries = [value for row in square for value in row]
is_normal = is_magic and sorted(entries) == list(range(1, n * n + 1))
print("Rows:", rows)
print("Columns:", columns)
print("Diagonals:", diagonals)
print("Magic:", is_magic, "Normal:", is_normal)
assert lines == [1033] * 12
assert is_magic and not is_normal

Expected output:

Rows: [1033, 1033, 1033, 1033, 1033]
Columns: [1033, 1033, 1033, 1033, 1033]
Diagonals: [1033, 1033]
Magic: True Normal: False

The repository command python scripts/check_number_examples.py checks the same twelve lines alongside the recorded prime-number examples. Either calculation checks arithmetic, not provenance or historical server acceptance.

Where the magic constant comes from

If an order-n square has row sum M, adding all rows gives a total of nM. Thus the total of all entries divided by n is the only possible common row sum. For this example, the total is 5165, so the candidate is 5165 / 5 = 1033. That is a necessary condition, not a substitute for checking the individual lines.

For a normal square, the sum of the integers 1 through n² is n²(n² + 1) / 2. Dividing by n gives the familiar constant n(n² + 1) / 2. A normal order-5 square therefore has constant 65. Applying that formula to this non-normal historical arrangement would be a category error: it does not contain the integers 1 through 25.

Symmetry is a different property

The three-by-three interior in the signed payload looks balanced because it repeats under horizontal and vertical reflection. But its outer rows total 32 while its middle row totals 38. Its columns likewise total 32, 38, and 32; both main diagonals total 34. The total of all entries is 102, giving a candidate constant of 34, yet not one row reaches that value.

	Check	Historical 5 × 5	Payload interior 3 × 3

	All row sums equal?	Yes: 1033	No: 32, 38, 32

	All column sums equal?	Yes: 1033	No: 32, 38, 32

	Both main diagonals match that sum?	Yes: 1033	Both are 34, but the rows and columns fail

	Magic under the stated definition?	Yes	No


A centered layout, repeated numbers, or matching diagonals can be worth describing without calling the arrangement magic. The LP2-15 panel guide applies this same separation between visible pattern and checked sums to its sixteen printed values.
