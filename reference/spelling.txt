Spelling, rune choices, and prime sums

Exact arithmetic · Source spellings · What the numerical patterns establish

The exact rune sequence determines the number. A polished English rendering can conceal numerical structure by changing how several letters are represented. Some unusual spellings have numerical consequences; others are aliases, reorderings, or transcription errors.

The strongest example here is LP2-57: each of its three body sentences has a prime sum that reverses to another prime. We can verify that property. Whether each individual spelling was chosen specifically to produce it is a separate question.

1. Define the sum before interpreting it

For rune sequence r₁, …, rₙ, use S = GP(r₁) + … + GP(rₙ), where GP is the rune’s prime value in Gematria Primus. Count each rune once. These calculations exclude punctuation, spaces, line markers, and decimal digits.

Keep three choices explicit: which text (ciphertext or known decoded text), which boundary (word, sentence, or page), and which encoding (one multi-letter rune or several individual runes). Changing a choice changes the question being answered.

All examples below use the known decoded rune sequence. LP2-57 and LP1-16 are directly readable; WIDSOM on LP1-04 is taken from its documented keyed reading. The ordinary page sidebar’s prime sum instead describes the archived rune sequence.

2. LP2-57: sentence sums and emirps

An emirp is a prime whose decimal reversal is a different prime. The three body sentences, using the archive’s clause boundaries, have this property:

Text segment	Rune sum	Decimal reversal	Result
PARABLE — heading	449	944	449 is prime; 944 is composite
Like the instar tunneling to the surface.	1259	9521	Both prime; emirp
We must shed our own circumferences.	1031	1301	Both prime; emirp
Find the divinity within and emerge.	1229	9221	Both prime; emirp

The complete heading-plus-body total is 3968 = 2⁷ × 31, which is composite. “All numbers are prime” is therefore too broad. The boundary is part of the observation: the three body sentence sums are emirps; the whole-page sum is not prime.

Why expanding the English spelling changes the result

The “NG” rune can stand for NG or ING. In the archived direct reading, TUNNELNG corresponds to contextual English “tunneling.” Adding an I rune when re-encoding that English word adds 31. Keeping everything else fixed changes the first sentence from 1259 to 1290 = 2 × 3 × 5 × 43. The emirp property disappears.

This gives a concrete numerical reason to preserve the original encoding. It does not make the NG/ING alias a spelling mistake: one rune legitimately represents several Latin letters. Inspect the original sequence.

CWESTIAN / KWESTION: the exact encoding matters

The seven labels are C · W · E · S · T · IA · N. C/K and IA/IO are alternative Latin labels for the same runes, so CWESTIAN and KWESTION can describe the same rune sequence. Its sum is 13 + 19 + 67 + 53 + 59 + 107 + 29 = 347, a prime.

For an explicit comparison, keep C W E S T and N fixed, but replace the single IA/IO rune (107) with separate I and O runes (31 + 7). The total becomes 347 − 107 + 31 + 7 = 278 = 2 × 139. This alternate rune encoding is composite. It is a counterfactual comparison, not text found on the page.

The conventional spelling QUESTION cannot be assigned a unique GP sum without an additional spelling rule: the chart has no separate Q rune. Quietly converting Q into some preferred sequence would build an assumption into the result. We can establish the printed encoding’s prime sum without claiming that this proves why the author selected it.

WIDSOM / WISDOM: reordering cannot tune an additive sum

Both use the same six rune values and sum to 270 = 2 × 3³ × 5. Swapping D and S changes their order, not their sum. The same holds for every larger word or sentence total containing that swap while keeping its boundary fixed.

Formally, for any permutation π, Σ GP(rᵢ) = Σ GP(rπ(i)). An order-sensitive cipher can distinguish the spellings; an additive prime sum cannot. WIDSOM is preserved in the known keyed reading of LP1-04, while WISDOM appears in LP1-05. Neither word total is prime.

If a calculation adds a delimiter value of 1, then 270 + 1 = 271, which is prime. That is a different counting convention, and it gives the same result for both word orders. The rune-only chart does not supply this extra 1. A punctuation-inclusive claim must state and justify its delimiter rule rather than quietly add it after seeing the total.

BELIEUE / BELEIUE: a transcription correction, not prime fitting

The native-image correction on LP1-10 restores BELIEUE from the archived I/E reversal. Both orders sum to 369 = 3² × 41. The correction matters for fidelity and sequence-sensitive work, but it cannot make an unchanged additive word or sentence sum prime. The U/V rendering in contextual BELIEVE does not add another rune.

FUNCTIAN, ILLUSIANS, SUFFERNG: aliases can look like omissions

IA/IO and NG/ING account for these literal forms. Their rune sums are 242, 422, and 217, all composite. Replacing IA/IO with separate I and O decreases the first two sums by 69, producing 173 and 353, both prime. Adding I before NG in SUFFERNG produces 248, composite.

These contrasting examples matter: the observed spelling does not universally maximize word-level primality. A claim about “fitting the primes” must name the intended boundary and evaluate the complete specified set, including examples that do not fit.

4. What can we say about purpose?

Established numerical property: the three LP2-57 body sentences have emirp sums under the stated rune-only convention. CWESTIAN’s recorded rune sequence has a prime word sum. The calculations are reproducible.

Numerical consequence: some plausible alternative encodings change these properties. Pure reorderings and alias relabelings leave additive sums unchanged. Transcription mistakes must be corrected from source evidence, not selected to improve a number.

Proposed purpose: exact wording and rune choices may have been constrained to produce particular totals, possibly as structural markers or consistency checks. These are interpretations of the pattern, not an authenticated explanation of intent. The known text’s statements about sacred primes provide relevant context, but do not specify a universal spelling-selection rule.

A useful test fixes the target unit, segmentation, punctuation values, and allowed alternatives before counting. Count all matches and non-matches. Searching many boundaries, aliases, reversals, and offsets creates many opportunities to find a prime; a selected hit alone does not quantify how surprising it is. No calibrated probability of authorial intent is claimed here.
